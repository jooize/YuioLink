2 · Bold labels, plus the fact that outranks them

Yes — and it is a better answer than mine. Bolding the keys makes weight mean navigation rather than authority, which frees the page to settle authority in words instead of typography. Typography can only ever imply "trust this"; a sentence can say which part is checkable and which part is somebody's assertion. We inform, we don't judge.

Pick

Labels bold, note beneath

magnet: ?xt=urn:btih:c12fe3a94b81d7e05f2c6a9048bb3e1d7f4a2c60&dn=ubuntu-24.04-desktop-amd64.iso&tr=udp://tracker.example.org:6969 Only xt identifies the data. dn is a name the link's creator chose, and it does not have to match what arrives.

Two sentences, no verdict. It states what the standard says and stops — it never claims the file is bad, only that the name is not evidence. A visitor who reads it can decide for themselves; one who doesn't is no worse off than before.

Longer, more explicit

magnet: ?xt=urn:btih:c12fe3a94b81d7e05f2c6a9048bb3e1d7f4a2c60&dn=ubuntu-24.04-desktop-amd64.iso A magnet names data by its hash, so xt is the only field that decides what you get. dn is a display name written by whoever made the link, and tr lists trackers to ask. YuioLink cannot check any of them.

Teaches the whole format. Against: four lines of prose on a page that already carries a hedge and a caution, for a scheme most visitors will meet once. The short version says the load-bearing half.

Worth saying which schemes get a note at all: only where the standard creates a gap between what is shown and what is true. magnet has one (name versus hash). mailto with a subject or body has a smaller one — those are pre-written words that would be sent in your name. tel, sms, xmpp, irc, matrix have none: the value is the thing. A note that appears on every page is furniture; one that appears on two is information.

4 · Formatting numbers — there is a safe way

My "deciding reason" last round was wrong. I said grouping digits makes two different numbers look alike. It doesn't — grouping removes nothing, and +47 800 123 45 is exactly as distinguishable from +47 800 123 46 as the ungrouped pair. The real objection is narrower, and it survives: a literal space is a hazard we now mark, and any display that normalises spacing would erase the tell.
A

As stored, tabular digits

tel: +4780012345

Truthful and slightly hostile to read. Eleven digits with no rhythm is exactly the string people mis-scan.

B · Pick

Grouped with margins, not characters

tel: +4780012345

The text node is untouched — the gaps are margin-left on spans, so the DOM still reads +4780012345 and ⌘C copies exactly that. Nothing is inserted, nothing is hidden, and the string on screen is character-for-character the string in the database. Guard: apply it only when the stored body contains no literal spaces of its own, so grouping can never disguise padding.

C

Formatted, with the true string beneath

tel: +47 800 12 345 stored as +4780012345

Shows both, so nothing is hidden — but it needs the country tables to know that Norway groups 8 digits as 3-2-3, and those tables are large, go stale, and would be the first per-country data in the codebase. It also puts two versions of one fact on the page and makes the visitor decide which to check.

On the grouping itself: B groups from the left in threes, which is a rhythm rather than a claim — it does not assert that 800 is an area code, because nothing tells the reader it is. That's the honest ceiling without country data. If you want real per-country grouping later, C is the shape it takes, and the "stored as" line is what keeps it truthful.
