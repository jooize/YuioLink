YuioLink · design note 9

Assembled: plate, two-line button, boxed arrow, lid

Taking your calls: B without dimming, description on the button, lid kept (unusual schemes are the surprise, and the surprise is the reason), and the hedge line underneath.

1 · B, with the body at full strength

The mix read as messy because two bold objects at similar sizes were competing. Keep one marked object — the scheme — and let the body simply be legible.

Was

Body dimmed

mailto: hello@example.com magnet: ?xt=urn:btih:c12fe3…9ab4&dn=ubuntu-24.04

The address is the thing being checked, and it is the faintest text in the card. Wrong emphasis.

Pick

Body at full text colour

mailto: hello@example.com magnet: ?xt=urn:btih:c12fe3…9ab4&dn=ubuntu-24.04

Same 15px, same weight, just not greyed. One object is marked (the scheme), one is plainly readable (the value), and nothing competes. This also matches the URL line, where .qv — the part that carries meaning — is full text colour while separators stay quiet.

Bolder

Body bold at 17px

mailto: hello@example.com magnet: ?xt=urn:btih:c12fe3…9ab4&dn=ubuntu-24.04

Fine for an email address; the magnet hash turns into a slab. If you want more presence, this is the ceiling — past it the mix goes messy again.

2 · The button label, in two lines

You're right that "Open magnet: Link" is jargon to anyone who has never met the word scheme. The fix isn't to guess what the device does — it's to use the everyday verb where the standard names the thing itself, and fall back to Open …: Link only where no such verb exists.

Pick

Everyday verb where one exists

Email hello@example.com An email address Call +47 800 12 345 A phone number Open magnet: Link A file identified by its hash

"Email hello@example.com" is not a prediction. It names what the link is for — RFC 6068 defines mailto: as an email address — and it says nothing about which app appears. The hedge line below the button is what covers the device. Where no plain verb is defined (magnet, ftp, irc), Open …: Link is honest rather than awkward: there genuinely is no everyday word for it.

Uniform

Open …: Link throughout

Open mailto: Link An email address Open tel: Link A phone number Open magnet: Link A file identified by its hash

One template, nothing to get wrong — but it asks every visitor to learn the word scheme in order to read a button, and the second line is already doing the teaching.

Verb only

No scheme word at all

Email hello@example.com An email address Call +47 800 12 345 A phone number Open This File Link A file identified by its hash

Friendliest, and the least true: "Open This File Link" is a paraphrase we invented, and magnet: does not have to point at a file. The scheme word is the one label that is guaranteed accurate.

3 · Yes — this is where the boxed arrow belongs

You asked, and it resolves the thing that was bothering me about it. openIcon's comment in app.js says "opens elsewhere", which I narrowed to "new tab" last round. Elsewhere is the better reading, and it splits cleanly.

Bare arrow — another site, same browser

Tier 1 · http / https https://example.com/products/keycaps Continue to example.com

You stay in the browser and arrive somewhere you can read. The plain external-link arrow, and the page you land on is still a page.

Pick

Boxed arrow — out of the browser entirely

Tier 2 · everything else on the allowlist mailto: hello@example.com Email hello@example.com An email address

The arrow leaving a frame says the destination is not a page in this browser — which is exactly the distinction the whole tier exists for. One honest tension: the history row uses this glyph for a target="_blank" link, which is still the browser. The common thread is "somewhere other than here", which is what the comment already says, but the two uses are not identical.

4 · The lid, landing the button in its own place

Click one. Lid and button share a single grid cell and are both 54px, so the button appears exactly where the lid was — the pointer is already on it. Still a plain <details>, still no JavaScript, and the anchor is genuinely out of the tab order while the lid is down.

Pick

Not a web address

mailto: hello@example.com
Not a web addressShow… Email hello@example.com An email address
What opens it, if anything, is up to your device.

Plainly true, and it names the surprise directly — a shortener is a web thing, and this one isn't. Earlier I ruled this phrase out, but that was for the refusal panel, where it would have implied mailto: is unwelcome. Here it is simply the fact.

Leaves the browser

tel: +47 800 12 345
Leaves the browserShow… Call +47 800 12 345 A phone number

Stronger about consequence, weaker about fact — on a device with nothing registered, pressing leaves nothing at all. "Not a web address" cannot be wrong.

Unusual scheme

Names your reason exactly — you don't expect these in a shortener. Against: "unusual" is a judgement about the visitor's world, and mailto: is not unusual to anyone.
